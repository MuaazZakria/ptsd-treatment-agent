Exploring MDMA assisted therapy in eating disorders: mechanisms, clinical evidence, and future directions

Authors: Michael Harkhoe, T. Offringa, E. Vermetten
Journal: Journal of Eating Disorders
Date: 2025
DOI: 10.1186/s40337-025-01409-5
Citations: 0

ABSTRACT: Patients with eating disorders (EDs), particularly anorexia nervosa (AN), experience a complex psychiatric condition often characterized by extreme food restriction, intense fear of weight gain, elevated levels of emotional dysregulation, body image disturbance, and comorbid trauma. Several of these factors can undermine the therapeutic alliance and reduce engagement with treatment, contributing to poorer outcomes. MDMA, a non-classical psychedelic, is being explored as a novel PTSD treatment adjuvans due to its ability to rapidly reduce trauma symptoms and enhance therapeutic alliance. Recent clinical trials and regulatory considerations, as highlighted in emerging research, are shaping its potential therapeutic role, and MDMA may offer a unique mechanism to disrupt maladaptive neural circuits, enhance cognitive flexibility, and facilitate emotional processing in EDs. To comprehensively evaluate the potential of MDMA-assisted therapy for EDs with a particular focus on the distinct neurobiological and psychological profiles of AN and comorbid PTSD. This paper synthesizes current research literature on MDMA, PTSD, and EDs, with an emphasis on clinical trial outcomes, neurobiological mechanisms, and therapeutic frameworks. Both pharmacological and psychotherapeutic components of MDMA-AT are reviewed. No clinical trials of MDMA-AT have been conducted in ED populations to date. Findings from clinical trials in patients with PTSD suggest that MDMA’s pro-social and fear-reducing and neuroplastic properties may enhance emotional processing, therapeutic alliance, and cognitive flexibility - key factors that often hinder eating disorder treatment. The ability of MDMA to increase emotional openness, reduce fear responses, and promote cognitive flexibility could support deeper engagement with the therapeutic process and improve treatment outcomes in EDs with comorbid trauma. The current evidence base suggests that MDMA-AT may hold promise as an adjunctive treatment for EDs echoing its demonstrated therapeutic potential in PTSD. By facilitating deeper emotional processing, enhancing patient-therapist attunement, and fostering openness to change, MDMA may help overcome avoidance, cognitive rigidity, and therapeutic impasses that often hinder progress in EDs. Its integration into clinical practice will require rigorous validation through well powered trials, alongside careful ethical and regulatory oversight, and integration into multidisciplinary treatment frameworks. Tailored dosing, patient selection, and therapist training will be essential for safe and effective implementation. Further research is warranted to fully explore this potential application. People with eating disorders often deal with complex mental health issues, including difficulty managing emotions, negative body image, and past trauma. An eating disorder may, perversely, be functional for a patient with regard to coping with these issues and may therefore be difficult for a patient to ‘let go.’ This can make it difficult for patients to connect with their therapist and stay committed to treatment, which makes treatment less effective. MDMA-assisted therapy is being studied as a novel way to treat PTSD, because it has shown to quickly reduce trauma symptoms and help build a stronger bond between patient and therapist. Since MDMA helps people feel more connected and less afraid, it might also be helpful for treating eating disorders—especially since avoiding emotions and dealing with trauma are common in these conditions. Although this idea shows promise, more research is needed to fully understand the potential of MDMA-assisted therapy for patients with eating disorders.

FULL TEXT:
Eating disorders (EDs) are potentially severe psychiatric illnesses characterized by abnormal eating patterns, a lower quality of life and high rates of morbidity and mortality. The most common EDs are anorexia nervosa (AN), bulimia nervosa (BN) and binge eating disorder (BED), with a lifetime prevalence estimated to be around 8.4% for women and 2.2% for men [1, 2]. Patients with EDs often suffer from various comorbidities, including obsessive compulsive disorder (OCD), Depression [3], Substance use disorders [4] and post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD) [5–8]. While ED behaviors may be conceptualized as maladaptive coping behaviors for trauma symptoms in some cases, there are complex mechanisms involved that have yet to be fully delineated.

Current treatment for patients with EDs consists of psychotherapy focused on behavioral patterns, sometimes combined with pharmacological interventions [9]. For adolescents and children specifically, the first-choice therapy for AN is family based-therapy, while Cognitive behavioral therapy enhanced (CBT-E) is the first-choice therapy for adults with AN, and patients with BN and BED regardless of age [10]. Pharmacological interventions can be effective in reducing ED-symptoms. Antidepressants such as sertraline or fluvoxamine may reduce binge- eating and purging in BN and BED [11–13]. Olanzapine, an atypical antipsychotic, can reduce eating related thoughts and sleeping problems, and increase concentration in patients with AN [14–16].

The current treatment for PTSD primarily consists of trauma-focused therapies, such as trauma-focused cognitive behavioral therapy (CBT), prolonged exposure (PE) and cognitive processing therapy (CPT). These are sometimes delivered in combination with Eye Movement Desensitization and Reprocessing (EMDR) or pharmacological interventions [17, 18]. While pharmacological options – most commonly antidepressants – are widely used, their efficacy remains limited compared to psychotherapy [19–22]. Prolonged exposure therapy helps patients directly confront trauma-related memories, feelings and situations they typically avoid. By systematically reducing avoidance for addressing fear responses, PE can diminish core PTSD symptoms and associated distress [23]. Cognitive processing therapy, by contract, emphasizes restructuring maladaptive trauma-related beliefs and appraisals, supporting patients to reinterpret the meaning of their traumatic experiences and reduce their negative impact on daily functioning [24]. EMDR is another structured psychotherapeutic approach specifically designed for the treatment of PTSD. It involves the recall of distressing or traumatic memories while the patient engages in bilateral stimulation – most often guided eye movements [25]. The leading explanatory model, the working memory theory [26], suggests that holding a traumatic memory in mind while simultaneously performing a competing task, taxes the limited capacity of the working memory system [27]. This dual-task condition reduces the vividness and emotional intensity of the traumatic memory, making it less distressing when recalled [28]. Through repeated application, EMDR facilitates the development of more adaptive cognitive appraisals of traumatic events, replacing maladaptive beliefs and attenuating the memory’s emotional charge [29].

Traumatic experiences and PTSD symptoms are common among individuals with EDs, with a pooled prevalence of PTSD in EDs of between 18–24% [6]. This varies significantly across ED subtypes, being 12% in AN, 26% in BED and 45% in BN [8, 30] Underlying traits that strengthen both ED behavior and PTSD symptoms include low self-worth, anxiety and self-criticism [31]. Patients with both EDs and comorbid PTSD report greater ED symptoms, have a worse prognosis on average and have more therapy resistant EDs [32, 33]. Furthermore, treatment of underweight patients with EDs and comorbid PTSD can be more difficult and less successful as a result of complications from low body weight and malnutrition, including decreased concentration, slowed mental processing and emotional numbness [8, 34].

Another complication in the treatment of patients with EDs and comorbid PTSD is the interaction between ED symptoms and PTSD symptoms. As EDs can be described as maladaptive coping mechanisms, increased PTSD symptoms may also worsen ED symptoms in patients with EDs and comorbid PTSD [35, 36]. Simultaneously, when patients undergo ED treatment, they lose their main coping strategy and can experience an increase in PTSD symptoms [37]. This bidirectional relationship makes treating ED patients with comorbid PTSD complex. Treating these patients therefore asks for an integrated treatment, combining intensive ED and PTSD treatment, in which patients can work on their trauma, while supporting them in their struggle against the ED. Recent studies have shown this integrated treatment to be safe and effective in improving both ED and PTSD symptoms in patients [38–40].

While PTSD requires intensive trauma therapy such as CBT or EMDR, the presence of ED pathology can significantly hinder treatment effectiveness. ED related behaviors may function as maladaptive coping strategies for regulating overwhelming emotions and trauma-related distress, thereby obstructing the development of adaptive emotion regulation skills that are essential for successful PTSD therapy [31]. Moreover, malnutrition and restrictive eating patterns, particularly in conditions like AN, can lead to emotional numbing and impair concentration and reduced cognitive flexibility, all of which limit a patients capacity to engage in trauma-focused interventions [34, 41]. In addition, individuals with EDs frequently struggle with heightened shame, avoidance, and in some cases denial of illness, ultimately reducing treatment adherence and engagement [42, 43].

This ongoing clinical challenge requires development of novel and integrative approaches, which brings the hypothesis of 3,4 methylenedioxymethamphetamine (MDMA) adjuvant trauma therapy into consideration. Psychedelic-assisted therapies have demonstrated efficacy in clinical trials for the treatment of common comorbid psychiatric disorders such as mood disorders [44, 45], PTSD [46], and substance-use disorders [47–49]. As an adjuvant, MDMA has shown therapeutic benefit in a phase 3 clinical trial for patients with PTSD, suggesting that MDMA may enhance effectiveness of trauma therapy in individuals whole eating disorder pathology reduces stress tolerance and coping flexibility [50–52]. In previous work by Brewerton et al., the theoretical potential of MDMA for EDs with comorbid PTSD was explored [53]. The authors proposed that MDMA-assisted therapy (MDMA-AT) may simultaneously alleviate ED and PTSD symptoms in patients presenting with both conditions. Building on that conceptual foundation, the current paper aims to further investigate the role of MDMA as an adjuvant in trauma therapy for patients with EDs and PTSD, with a particular focus on how MDMA-AT could be adapted for this population. First, we will examine the pharmacological effects of MDMA and its role in trauma therapy. Next, we will discuss its potential for treating comorbid PTSD and EDs, and finally, we will outline a research agenda with special considerations for MDMA-AT for patients with EDs.

MDMA was originally synthesized in 1912 by Merck Pharmaceuticals as a precursor for hemostatic agents, with its effect on the central nervous system not being recognized till some decades later. By the 1970 s, MDMA was being used recreationally for its pleasurable, prosocial effects, which enhance social bonding and emotional openness [54, 55]. MDMA primarily acts on neurotransmitter transporters for serotonin (SERT), dopamine (DAT), and norepinephrine (NET) [56]. MDMA’s key mechanism involves reverse serotonin transport or serotonin efflux through SERT, increasing extracellular serotonin levels, leading to increased activation of postsynaptic serotonin receptors and downstream signaling pathways [57]. Unlike selective serotonin reuptake inhibitors (SSRIs), which block serotonin reuptake, MDMA facilitates serotonin release directly through the transporter, resulting in a more rapid increase in serotonin levels compared to SSRI usage, while also competing with serotonin for uptake by SERT, further increasing synaptic serotonin levels [58].

Oxytocin has emerged as a key neuropeptide implicated in the therapeutic effects of MDMA. Research has demonstrated that MDMA elevates oxytocin levels, which has been strongly associated with prosocial behavior, emotional bonding, trust, and the reinforcement of social reward learning [59, 60]. Although the precise neural mechanisms underlying the social reward system remain incompletely understood, oxytocin is known to facilitate the positive reinforcement of social interactions [61, 62]. A key concept in understanding these effects is the notion of a ´critical period´, a developmental window during which the brain exhibits heightened plasticity and behavioral malleability [63]. In the context of social reward learning, this critical period is regulated in part by oxytocin, which reaches peak activity in early childhood and typically declines before adulthood [63]. Notably, preclinical studies in mice suggest that MDMA can reactivate or prolong this critical period in adult mice, by stimulating oxytocin receptors within the nucleus accumbens [64]. This reactivation appears to enhance social reward learning, thereby providing a potential explanation for MDMA´s therapeutic utility in disorders characterized by social dysfunction [65].

In addition to its prosocial effects, MDMA is also known to attenuate fear and anxiety responses, a property attributed to its modulatory effects on amygdala activity [66]. The amygdala, together with the medial prefrontal cortex and hippocampus, plays a central role in the neural circuitry of fear acquisition, processing and sensitization [67]. By dampening amygdala hyperactivity and enhancing prefrontal-amygdala connectivity and facilitating hippocampal contextual processing, MDMA may disrupt the persistence of conditioned fear responses. These effects are thought to be mediated by serotonergic (5HT2A/5HT1A), oxytocinergic, and noradrenergic pathways, which modulate salience and bonding [64, 68]. Together, these neurobiological actions provide a plausible basis for MDMA’s therapeutic potential as an adjunct to psychotherapy in the treatment of PTSD [52].

Despite its widespread use as a recreational psychoactive substance, MDMA carries significant acute and long term health risks [69, 70]. One of the most severe complications is acute hyperthermia, a disruption of the thermoregulation of the body, which if untreated can result in multi-organ failure [71]. Other common short term side effects include post-use lethargy, irritability and dysphoria, which can be attributed to temporary serotonin depletion following MDMA exposure [72]. While these complications typically occur during or in the immediate days after use, evidence also indicated that chronic and high-dose recreational MDMA consumption may induce neurotoxicity of serotonergic neurons, potentially contributing to mood disturbances and cognitive impairments such as memory deficits [73]. These risks are further amplified when MDMA is consumed in combination with other psychoactive substances, particularly alcohol, amphetamines or SSRI’s [74–77].

However, when corrected for confounding factors, such as concomitant drug use, and use in a controlled clinical setting, studies report primarily mild and transient side effects associated with MDMA [78]. In the context of MDMA-AT, one phase 3 trial demonstrated that MDMA had a superior safety profile comparative to conventional pharmacological treatments such as paroxetine and sertraline [79]. Although MDMA as an adjuvant treatment option is still relatively new, it remains essential to implement risk-mitigation strategies, particularly with regards to maintain rigorous long-term monitoring of both therapeutic benefits and possible adverse effects.

MDMA-AT is an intervention that combines pharmacological effects of MDMA with structured psychotherapy. It is currently investigated for the treatment of PTSD, a disorder marked by impaired ability to extinguish conditioned fear responses, where it shown promising efficacy in patients with treatment resistant PTSD [46]. MDMA may facilitate therapeutic progress by inducing temporary reduction in fear, heightened positive emotions towards oneself and others, and increased interpersonal trust. These effects can increase the therapeutic engagement while reducing the likelihood of dissociation or emotionally numbing during sessions. Moreover, the increased trust in the therapist strengthens the therapeutic alliance, together contributing to improved treatment outcomes [80].

MDMA-AT sessions are conducted by clinicians who have received specialized training ensuring both patient safety and the optimization of therapeutic outcomes. Treatment typically begins with three non-drug preparation sessions, during which the clinician and participant work on establishing trust and building a strong therapeutic alliance. These sessions also help the participant learn to anticipate, navigate, and respond to distressing memories or emotions that may arise during MDMA sessions [50]. Following this preparation phase, participants enter the treatment period of MDMA-AT. During each drug-assisted session begin with individuals receiving an initial dose of 80-120 mg MDMA, which is followed by a supplemental dose of 60 mg after 1.5–2.5 h, designed to prolong the therapeutic window and maintain engagement in the therapeutic process [50, 81].

The dosing of MDMA in MDMA-AT is a critical factor that balances therapeutic efficacy with patient safety. Clinical trials have established that a safe and effective therapeutic starting dose ranges from 75 to 125 mg, with a supplemental dose of half the initial amount (e.g. 37,5–62,5 mg) [82, 83]. This split-dosing is designed to both prolong the empathogenic and prosocial effects of MDMA during the therapy session, while minimizing the risk of overstimulation or adverse effects [50]. This fixed-dose approach was done regardless of body weight of the participants. However, in patients with EDs, especially those with AN, the use of a fixed dose of MDMA may pose increased physiological risks, due to their typically low body weight and altered metabolic state. Fixed dosing, while effective in the general psychiatric population, does not account for the significantly reduced body mass and fat stores that influence the pharmacokinetics and distribution of MDMA [84]. In individuals with a low BMI, a standard dose of 125 mg could lead to higher plasma concentrations, increasing the risk of adverse effects, such as cardiovascular strain and dehydration, conditions to which patients with AN are often more vulnerable to, due to potential electrolyte imbalances and impaired thermoregulation [85, 86]. In this population, weight-based dosing would offer a more individualized and physiologically safer approach.

During the sessions, therapists, adhering to standardized MDMA assisted therapy treatment protocols, support patients in processing traumatic experiences and exploring broader impact of PTSD symptoms on their lives [87]. Therapeutic sessions are typically scheduled at four-week intervals, with three 90-minute integration sessions conducted in the weeks between [50]. Spacing the sessions several weeks apart provides participants with time to reflect on and integrate insights gained during each session into their daily lives. The integration sessions further support psychological processing and deepen participants’ understanding of their experiences [50]. The intervals also give the brain the time to recover neurochemically, reducing the risks associated with serotonin depletion. This approach is supported by clinical trial protocols, such as those from the Multidisciplinary Association for Psychedelic studies (MAPS), which have demonstrated sustained symptom improvements using a similar schedule [87].

Given the growing body of evidence supporting the efficacy of MDMA-AT for the treatment of PTSD, it is possible that MDMA-AT may also hold therapeutic potential for individuals with EDs and comorbid PTSD. This hypothesis is supported by the high prevalence of PTSD amongst individuals with EDs and the overlap in underlying psychological mechanisms shared by both conditions [5, 31]. These shared constructs include chronic self-criticism, diminished self-worth, pervasive guilt and shame and emotional dysregulation [31, 88] Given MDMA-AT’s ability to facilitate emotional processing, reduce fear-based responses, and enhance self-compassion and therapeutic alliance in the treatment of PTSD, it may offer a novel and integrative intervention for targeting these shared transdiagnostic features in comorbid presentations of EDs and PTSD [55, 89].

MDMA´s ability to increase prosocial behaviors, such as empathy, trust, and emotional closeness, could be used to enhance the therapeutic alliance. These effects may be beneficial for patients with EDs and PTSD, who often suffer from interpersonal distrust and have a high dropout rate for treatment, with an estimated dropout rate ranging from 20% to 50% (inpatient) and 30% to 70% (outpatient) in the treatment of EDs [90, 91]. While the dropout rate for PTSD treatment is lower at 16–18%, MDMA-AT for PTSD treatment has an even lower dropout rate [46, 92]. This reduction in dropout rate could also occur in the treatment of patients with EDs and comorbid PTSD. By fostering a safe and supportive environment, MDMA-AT may reduce dropout rates and facilitate deeper engagement in therapy and more effective processing of traumatic experiences.

MDMA induces the release of neurotransmitters such as serotonin, dopamine, and oxytocin, which can promote neuroplasticity, the brain’s ability to reorganize itself by forming new neural connections [56, 63]. While the exact mechanisms underlying MDMA-induced neuroplasticity are still under investigation, current research indicates that MDMA enhances dendritic branching and increases synaptic density [93], while MDMA’s effect on neurogenesis can vary by dose, with high doses downregulating neurogenesis [94]. These changes may facilitate the reprocessing of traumatic memories and support the restructuring of maladaptivebehaviors associated with ED and PTSD pathology.

MDMA’s anxiolytic properties, attributed to its inhibitory action on neurons of the amygdala, can reduce fear and defensiveness, allowing individuals to confront and process traumatic memories more effectively [66, 67, 95]. Additionally, MDMA usage has been associated with increased self-compassion and reduced self-criticism, which are often impaired in individuals with EDs and PTSD [31]. MDMA-AT could facilitate these emotional processing enhancements, which may lead to more adaptive coping strategies and improved treatment outcomes.

Trauma exists on a spectrum, encompassing a range of experiences from acute, discrete events to chronic, pervasive exposures. This continuum suggests that the impact of trauma on individuals varies not only in intensity, but also in duration and context [96]. In individuals with EDs, early-life and complex trauma have been identified as significant etiological factors, with individuals with EDs often reporting histories of emotional, physical, or sexual abuse, and other forms of interpersonal trauma [97]. The severity and development of ED symptoms often correlates with the severity and timing of traumatic experiences. Early-life trauma for example, may interfere with the development of self-regulation and self-worth, which increases the chance of developing and ED, while chronic trauma exacerbates existing ED symptoms and complicates treatment efforts [98]. This relationship between trauma and EDs underscores the necessity for interventions that address both the trauma history and ED symptoms.

MDMA-AT has demonstrated efficacy in treating PTSD and emerging evidence suggests it may also be beneficial in the treatment of ED symptoms. A study involving participants with PTSD and ED symptoms reported significant reductions in ED symptoms. This improvement was reported in patients with and without active purging behaviors and low body weight, indicating that MDMA-AT may address ED pathology in individuals without an active ED diagnosis [99].

While the theoretical evidence of MDMA-AT for EDs looks promising, the available data is somewhat limited. At the moment of writing, there have been no clinical studies done on the effect of MDMA-AT on EDs or PTSD with comorbid EDs, with only one study done on ED symptoms in patients with PTSD. While MDMA-AT has been shown to be effective in the treatment of PTSD, the possible positive effects it can have on ED-PTSD treatment remain theoretical. Further exploratory investigations are needed to discover whether MDMA-AT could be an effective treatment option for patients with EDs and comorbid PTSD.

While the prevalence of PTSD amongst the different subtypes of EDs varies significantly, these subtypes could also differ in how they respond to MDMA-AT. Individuals with BN and BED, for example, show increased emotion dysregulation and impulsivity [100–103], while individuals with anorexia nervosa restrictive type (AN-R), show heightened emotional avoidance and greater cognitive rigidity [104]. This could present a challenge for individuals with AN, to engage in the emotional openness encouraged during MDMA-AT sessions. Another difference between the subtypes of EDs is the ego syntonic nature of AN. Individuals with AN often integrate their identity and self-worth with the ED, making it harder to challenge and teat the disorder [105]. Individuals with BED and BN however experience their ED as more ego dystonic, having a stronger wish to change, which makes them potentially more therapeutically accessible for MDMA-AT [106]. Lastly Individuals with AN are at a higher risk of somatic complications compared to individuals with BN and BED, largely due to the chronic and severe nutritional deficiencies associated with prolonged caloric restriction and low body weight [107]. Therefore, careful screening and medical assessment are essential, since MDMA-AT may not be suitable for individuals with severely low BMI’s or active medical instabilities. In contrast, patients with BED and BN are often more medically stable and may face fewer immediate risks from MDMA-AT.

While the MDMA-AT manualized protocol for PTSD offers a well-established framework, its direct application to eating disorder treatment may not fully capture the unique psychological and neurobiological complexities of EDs [87]. Therefore, adaptation may be necessary to tailor session content, duration, and therapeutic techniques to the specific challenges of ED patients, particularly those with AN. This could include greater emphasis on trust-building, motivational enhancement, and addressing body image distortions, as well as modified safety protocols for nutritional and metabolic vulnerabilities. Further research comparing the outcomes of the standard PTSD-based protocol versus a tailored ED-specific approach will be essential to determine the best practices. A staged research approach could compare early versus mid-phase introduction of MDMA to determine optimal timing.

MDMA-assisted therapy has demonstrated significant clinical potential in enhancing emotional openness and strengthening the therapeutic alliance, both recognized as critical predictors of successful treatment outcomes [80, 89]. While originally studied for PTSD, MDMA’s prosocial and emotional regulatory properties may also be beneficial in the treatment of patients with EDs. Patients with EDs often suffer from emotional rigidity, fear of (emotional) vulnerability and difficulties in interpersonal relationships, which can undermine therapeutic engagement and limit treatment success [108].

MDMA’s ability to strengthen social connections, enhance emotional openness, and reduce fear responses could address barriers to effective treatment in patients with EDs. Many individuals with EDs often also suffer from low self-esteem and negative body image. The combination of increased oxytocin release and heightened emotional openness induced by MDMA may help patients with EDs feel more secure in exploring vulnerable emotions and challenging maladaptive beliefs about body image and self-worth [63, 109]. This effect could increase receptivity to positive social cues and emotional validation, thereby reinforcing the development of adaptive coping mechanisms [55] Furthermore, the enhanced social reward sensitivity associated with MDMA usage may also counteract the negative reinforcement cycles that often sustain disordered eating behaviors, enabling patients to internalize healthier emotional and behavioral responses more effectively [110, 111]. One specific form of treatment for EDs in which MDMA could assist is body image therapy. Patients with EDs often suffer from body image issues such an increased focus on having a ‘perfect’ body, an overevaluation of weight and shape, upwards appearance comparisons, body checking and body image avoidance. Of these aspects, both body checking and body image avoidance have a maintaining effect on ED pathology [112]. Body image therapy aims to understand and reframe negative feelings and thoughts about one’s body, reducing the negative impact it has on one’s mental health [113]. The emotional openness, empathy and reduced defensiveness induced by MDMA may allow patients to feel safer in exploring vulnerable emotions and challenging maladaptive beliefs body-related beliefs. At the same time MDMA can enhance responsiveness to positive social cues and emotional validation during therapy, which helps in reinforcing new adaptive coping mechanisms [55]. The enhanced social reward sensitivity may also counteract the negative reinforcement cycles that often sustain disordered eating behaviors, helping patients internalize new emotional and behavioral responses more effectively. This oxytocin-mediated increase in emotional learning and reward processing could provide a critical window for restructuring maladaptive cognitive and emotional patterns in EDs.

A recent paper by Dunn et al., provides a comprehensive review of MDMA-AT, highlighting not only its established role in PTSD but also its potential applications in neurorehabilitation and cognitive recovery [114]. The paper explores evidence of MDMA’s unique profile suggesting that it could play a similar role in diverse clinical contexts such as neurorehabilitation, stroke recovery, and treatment of social cognitive deficits. These properties could improve not just emotional processing but also cognitive and functional recovery across neuropsychiatric conditions. This broadened perspective, grounded in rigorous review and analysis, sets the stage for cross-disciplinary research efforts aimed at integrating MDMA into innovative, patient-centered treatment models, maximizing its therapeutic impact across healthcare domains.

Building on this framework, we hypothesize that, MDMA’s combined prosocial, emotional, and neuroplastic effects may offer a novel treatment approach for EDs, particularly for patients with high emotional avoidance or comorbid trauma. Since the consequences of inadequately treated EDs are very severe, with AN having the highest mortality rate of all psychiatric disorders with 5.1 deaths per 1000 person/years, the potential benefits of MDMA as an adjunct treatment could be substantial [5, 115] Nevertheless, important questions remain regarding the therapeutic framework best suited to harness MDMA’s effects in the context of EDs.

To evaluate the feasibility of MDMA-AT for individuals with EDs, we recommend a phased research approach. Initial insights could be generated through user-focused research methods, such as focus groups or digital community platforms tailored to individuals with EDs. An example of such a platform is Proud2bme in the Netherlands, which has successfully engaged ED populations in peer-led support and discussions on treatment innovations [116, 117]. These exploratory methods can help identify key practical, ethical, and therapeutic considerations for implementing MDMA-AT in EDs. Building on this foundation subsequent phases should include early-phase clinical studies, such as open-label feasibility trials, to systematically assess safety, tolerability, and acceptability. These trials should incorporate exploratory outcomes related to emotional processing, therapeutic alliance, and symptom reduction, thereby establishing a foundation for later controlled studies.

If early feasibility studies show promising results, a pilot randomized controlled trial (RCT) could be conducted to evaluate the preliminary efficacy of MDMA-AT when used as a catalyzer for positive change in stagnated treatment processes. These studies could also help clarify the interaction between MDMA’s pharmacological effects and the therapeutic relationship, both of which are central to treatment outcomes. However, it is important to remain cautious when planning to adapt a new therapy like MDMA-AT for patients with EDs. There are several medical complications that could arise from MDMA usage that could worsen ED symptoms, such as nausea, loss of appetite and vomiting. These symptoms could increase the resistance patients feel towards food and eating, potentially further lowering their intake and worsening their physical status [118]. The sympathomimetic effects of MDMA could increase the risk of developing conditions such as cardiovascular disease and electrolyte imbalances. These risks are especially relevant in patients with EDs, who often are already at higher risk of developing cardiovascular and electrolyte disorders, due to behaviors like purging, restricting food intake and using diuretics or laxatives [119]. It is also important to be cautious of potential drug-drug interactions, particularly with medications that affect serotonin level, such as SSRI’s, SNRI’s and MAOI’, due to the increased chance of developing serotonin syndrome [120, 121]. With an estimated 70% of patients with EDs having a comorbid mood disorder diagnosis, clinicians need to be aware of these possible dangerous interactions [3, 4].

Looking further ahead, the integration of MDMA-AT in existing treatment protocols for Eds may represent a promising area for clinical innovation. Potential applications could include its use following medical stabilization and initial psychotherapy, as it can help foster trust and engagement, deepen engagement and address core psychological and neurobiological challenges. It could also theoretically be used during moments of plateau or treatment resistance to reignite progress and flexibility, or as an integration with adjunct treatments like family-based therapy or CBT-E to create a comprehensive and synergistic approach. In all scenarios these pathways require careful individualization, standardized protocols, continuous monitoring, and collaboration within multidisciplinary teams to ensure patient safety and maximize clinical benefit.

While theoretical evidence supports the therapeutic potential of MDMA for EDs and PTSD, considerable uncertainty remains. MDMA is known to induce strong prosocial and putative neuroplastic effects, both in combination psychotherapy and, in certain contexts, independently. What is not yet clear is the extent to which MDMA exerts a direct effect on ED pathology versus functioning as a catalyst within an existing therapeutic framework to facilitate change. Moreover, it remains to be determined which psychotherapeutic approaches are best suited to harness the unique emotional and cognitive effects of MDMA.

Drawing on the mechanistic pathways shared between EDs and PTSD and preliminary findings from a study of ED symptoms in PTSD populations treated with MDMA, it is hypothesized that MDMA-AT could offer novel strategies for addressing the complex emotional and cognitive patterns involved in EDs. However, clinical evidence in this specific population is currently absent, underscoring the need for rigorous research to evaluate the safety, efficacy, and long-term outcomes of MDMA-AT in this context.
